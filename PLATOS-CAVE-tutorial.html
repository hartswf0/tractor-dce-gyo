We Choose the Holes Before the Bricks
Start as if you were helping a brand‑new kid:

1. WHAT do you want to build?
  Pick one small story: an Ewok forest fight, a race, a harbor, or one band of the Shield (cities, war, harvest, rim).

2. What bags of LEGO do you already have?
  Open these tools in new tabs and make a few MPD "bags":
  - MINIFIGURATOR — minifig families / agents.
  - LOCATIONATOR — stages, sets, backgrounds.
  - VEHICULATOR — vehicles and rides.
  - COURAGE viewer — use the ★ star button to forage in its scene library.

For all of them, the safest loop is:
  - Load the MPD into Courage so you can orbit and sanity‑check it.
  - When it looks right, use Courage's COPY ALL MPD button.
  - That copied block of text is your bucket for the Architect.

Tiny, clean files work best at first: a single ramp test MPD (just a sloped walkway), a 6×6 marketplace, or one small vehicle. They are easy for Courage to show and easy for the Architect to reason about.

Then Plato's Cave runs the inverse 20 Questions game:
  - You bring those MPD buckets of parts.
  - The Architect decides which holes on a 9×9 tray they should fill.
  - After a few rounds, you get a compact MPD that still tells the story.

This tutorial walks one concrete example all the way through: a Moonlit Forest Skirmish with Ewoks vs. robots, so you can see how the Shield‑of‑Achilles reconstruction game will feel.

Say It Like a Story, Not a Spec
In the original spec you saw things like LEGOS-GPT, Micro-PLoT, and Global_Scene_Graph. Here's the same idea in plain language:

- LEGOS-GPT is the Site Surveyor.
  It reads your story (or Shield passage) and marks
  which squares on a 9×9 tray need something: city,   forest edge, war gate, vineyard, etc.

- Swiss is the Bucket Curator.
  It gives the Architect a handful of candidate parts   each round. The GPT never sees your whole collection   at once—just a little bag at a time.

- Micro-PLoT is a simple scoring game:
  G = geometry (can this part actually build a shape?)
  M = myth (does it fit the story in this zone?)
  W = weight (is it visually important or background?)

The GPT uses this to decide: "Claim this part for a
vignette, leave that part in the bucket."

Because you always paste real MPD text from Courage instead of describing parts in English, each round slowly saturates the GPT's context window with evidence: it can't invent bricks or filenames that don't exist; it has to choose from the bucket you gave it. Over many buckets this makes it a kind of arbiter of truth over your collection—creative about arrangement, but honest about which parts are actually on the table.

The GPT Starts with Shadows, Not Parts
Before you even hand the Architect a single MPD file, it can sketch a void map for the forest fight:

- {T} = tree anchors (north/south/east/west).
- [C] = empty clearing cells (stage).
- [L] = fallen log line (divide two sides).
- [E] = Ewok cluster.
- [R] = robot wedge.
- [X] = trap cue.

This is the "Plato's Cave" part: we are only looking at shapes and roles, not yet at part numbers. A kid can already see where "something" should go.

From Portrait Grid to Two Small Armies
When you paste the minifig_family.mpd bucket into the GPT, it doesn't try to stuff all 25 figures into the clearing. It:

- Sees the file as a family lattice (5×5). - Scores each member using Micro-PLoT (G, M, W). - CLAIMS 8 of them as actual fighters (4 Ewoks, 4 robots). - Leaves the rest as implied family, or does not place them.

This is the "Gatehouse of 12 instead of Wall of 100" rule: a few strong figures tell the story better than a crowd.

The Forest Grows, the World Scales Up
In later rounds you give the Architect two more buckets:

- Forest bucket with real tree parts, stumps, bushes, crates, wall panels, doors.
- Vehicle bucket with trains, boats, planes, cars.

The GPT:
- Builds a ring of mixed trees (palms, stumps, columnars, pyramids) so the forest isn't a monoculture.
- Adds a few crates on the robot side as hard cover.
- Parks one train at the east rim as the arrival vector.
- Puts one plane overhead as the "eye in the sky".

Your grid now has: a clear center stage, two teams, a forest boundary, and a sense that a whole world exists beyond the edge.

How a Kid Could Use This for the Shield
Once you've played through the Ewok forest example, the Shield-of-Achilles version is just a story swap:

1. Read the Shield text together and mark 3–5 big regions: sun, cities, war, harvest, Oceanus.
2. Draw a 9×9 grid on paper and let the child place icons where each region lives.
3. Use SWISS to make small buckets: "city buildings", "harvest pieces", "war pieces"...
4. For each bucket, paste it into the Architect and ask: "Which hole on our paper tray should these fill?"
5. Paste the MPD the GPT gives you into GRACE, orbit, and talk about what still feels empty.

The point is not to build every tree or every soldier. It is to build the essential form and let the story in the text (and the kid's imagination) do the rest.
